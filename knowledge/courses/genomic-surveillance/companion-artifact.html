From DNA to Decisions

A pathogen genome is a clock and a family tree at the same time. This is the machinery that turns one into epidemiological evidence — and the places where it quietly stops being true.

The spine

Every claim in genomic surveillance answers one of four questions. And what decides whether the answer is trustworthy is almost never the sequencer — it is the sampling and the metadata.

01 · The sorting reflex

Four questions, four kinds of evidence

Sort every claim into one of these before arguing about methods. Each owes you a different kind of evidence, and answering one question with another question's evidence is the most common error in the field. The colours are the four bases as a sequencing trace draws them; they recur in every figure below.

02 · The chain

Every step throws something away

The physical and computational chain from a molecule to a decision. Information is only ever lost moving rightward, so the skill is knowing what each step discarded and whether your question needed it. The two boxes that determine all of it — sampling and metadata — sit outside the chain.

03 · The calculation to run first

What can this pathogen's genome possibly resolve?

Genomic epidemiology works because a pathogen's genome changes at roughly the speed the pathogen spreads. One multiplication tells you, before any money is spent, whether sequencing can distinguish individual transmissions, only clusters, or only broad lineages. Run it at the start of every study.

04 · Phylodynamics

Why the shape of a tree is data about an epidemic

Run time backwards. Two lineages sampled today had a common ancestor at some point in the past, and how far back depends on how many infections there were to choose from. That inverse relationship is the whole engine of phylodynamics: node times are a noisy measurement of effective population size through time.

The hazard that eats phylodynamics

A change in sampling looks exactly like a change in dynamics. Fewer recent samples means fewer recent coalescences means Ne appears to fall — the epidemic looks controlled because surveillance weakened. The fix is a nested-truncation analysis: re-run the whole thing on progressively longer windows of your own data, with identical curation at each cutoff, and show the answer is stable.

05 · The limit

The transmission tree is not the phylogenetic tree

Three mechanisms separate a tree of sequences from a tree of people, and all three operate at once: incomplete sampling, within-host diversity creating a pre-transmission interval, and simply not enough mutations per link to order what happened.

Strength 1 · the strong one

Exclusion

"These isolates differ by 27 substitutions. Given a rate of ~16 per genome per year, this is inconsistent with direct transmission during the outbreak period."
Strength 2 · the honest middle

Consistency

"These isolates are identical. This is consistent with direct transmission, and equally consistent with a short chain through unsampled cases or with two infections from a common source."
Strength 3 · the one people skip

Indeterminacy

"Coverage was 71%, and the differentiating positions fall in a dropout region. These sequences cannot be compared at the resolution required."

The word confirmed should almost never appear in a genomic linkage statement. Genomics confirms exclusions. It supports, is consistent with, or fails to exclude everything else.

06 · Signal or artefact

When the host writes the mutations

Your immune system attacks pathogen genomes chemically. Those edits look exactly like mutations to a sequencing pipeline, but they are host-imposed damage rather than the pathogen's own replication error — and whether you use them or delete them depends entirely on whether the pathogen has an adequate clock of its own.

Same class of biology, opposite treatment. In the 2026 Bundibugyo analysis, 32 of 626 genomes were deleted for carrying ADAR signatures — and the removal was iterative, because a bout of editing on an internal branch affects multiple tips, so the outliers are not independent of each other.

Why "nothing has changed" is a result

There is no licensed vaccine or specific therapeutic for Bundibugyo virus — it is a distinct species from Zaire ebolavirus, which is what ERVEBO and the licensed monoclonals target. So the entire response rested on diagnostics, isolation and contact tracing. Finding that phylogenetic diversity was maintained and increasing, with no fitness-altering mutations and no lineage sweeping to dominance, is what told the response those three things still worked. It cost as much work as a positive finding.

08 · Before it is funded

The four questions that decide whether it is surveillance

A genomic surveillance programme can be technically excellent and epidemiologically useless. Sequences accumulate, trees are built, dashboards update — and no decision is different from what it would have been. Ask these in order.

#AskAnd if you cannot answer it
1What decision could this change, and who makes it?You are doing research — legitimate, but fund and evaluate it as research.
2What result would change it, and in which direction? Stated in advance.You have interpretation-after-the-fact. Almost every activity fails this one.
3By when must the answer arrive? Work backwards from the decision point.The result may be excellent and arrive after the decision, at which point it is worth nothing.
4What confidence is needed, and does the sampling support it?A proportion needs a representative frame; detection needs roughly 3/p sequences.

And the hardest professional skill in the field is saying that sequencing is the wrong tool: when the clock is too slow for the question, when the question is about magnitude rather than relatedness, when the decision does not depend on the answer, or when the same money spent on metadata would buy more epidemiology. In a programme where a large share of cases have no recorded onset date, the opportunity cost of a sequencer is a data manager — and the data manager frequently wins.

09 · The counter-example · T. b. gambiense

The disease where most of this does not apply

Every case above shows genomic surveillance working. This one mostly does not, and it is here deliberately — a course that only shows successes teaches the tools but not the judgement. Gambiense sleeping sickness is targeted for zero transmission by 2030, verified by five consecutive years of zero reported human cases. The parasite that causes it is clonal and monophyletic: every isolate looks like every other.

QThe questionApplies to gHAT?Why
AIs this the same outbreak?NoInsufficient diversity to resolve chains, clusters or links — at any sequencing depth
CWhere did it come from?Only with a baselineImport vs residual is answerable if historical sequences from that focus exist
GHow fast is it spreading?NoPhylodynamics needs coalescent signal in a time-scaled tree; there is not enough to build one
THas the pathogen changed?Yes — the value is hereSubspecies identification, drug-resistance markers as acoziborole scales, diagnostic-target integrity
The sampling frame and the reservoir are in different tissues

Trypanosome DNA is found in the blood of 67% of confirmed cases but only 9% of unconfirmed seropositives — and in the skin of up to 71% of confirmed and 41% of unconfirmed seropositives. Unconfirmed seropositives are never treated. The standard specimen misses much of the reservoir in exactly the group that matters most for elimination. A blood-only sample bank is a bank of the wrong compartment.

The one operational recommendation in the whole course

Archive before elimination. Once a focus reaches zero and a case appears in year three of the five-year clock, exactly one question matters: importation, or residual transmission that was never interrupted? They demand opposite responses. Genomes answer it — but only against a baseline collected beforehand. You cannot go back and sequence the transmission you have just successfully stopped.
