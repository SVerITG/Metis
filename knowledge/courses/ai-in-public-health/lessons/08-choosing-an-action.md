## Why this matters

Every other shape produces a **number**. This one produces a **choice** — who gets screened, who gets the drug, where the team goes, who is enrolled. That difference has three consequences, and all three are uncomfortable.

1. **The objective function encodes values, whether or not anyone wrote them down.** Maximising cases detected, cases detected per euro, and deaths averted give different answers, and choosing between them is a political act performed in a config file.
2. **You cannot evaluate it the way you evaluate a prediction.** To know whether a policy is good you would have to run it. Almost nobody does; they estimate it from data generated by a *different* policy, and that estimation is where the field's most spectacular failures live.
3. **It is the shape regulators care about.** A number is information; an action is a medical decision. See Lesson 10.

✱ And the honest framing for the whole lesson: **shape 6 is where health AI's claims are largest and its evidence is thinnest.** Not because the methods are bad, but because the evaluation is genuinely hard in a way the other shapes are not.

## Section 1 · A threshold is a value judgement

Turning a probability into an action requires a threshold, and the threshold *is* the ethics.

The formal statement is clean: if you would accept treating **N** people unnecessarily to catch one true case, your threshold probability is `pt = 1/(N+1)`. Accepting 9 unnecessary treatments per case caught means acting at 10%; accepting 99 means acting at 1%.

So `pt` is not tuned, it is **elicited** — from how bad the intervention is, how bad the missed case is, and who bears each. And it differs by setting: the same model, at the same calibration, should act at different thresholds in a well-resourced hospital and a rural clinic, because the costs of both errors differ.

⚠ A vendor supplying a single fixed threshold has made your value judgement for you, using their assumptions about your setting. Recall from the imaging deep dive that a **tunable** threshold was one of the seven conditions that separated TB CAD from dermatology apps.

## Section 2 · Net benefit — and a model that is worse than nothing

Sensitivity and specificity live on different axes and cannot be traded without a rate of exchange. Net benefit supplies one:

> `NB = TP/n − (FP/n) × pt/(1−pt)`

The weight `pt/(1−pt)` is the exchange rate implied by your threshold. Two reference strategies anchor the scale: **treat nobody** scores exactly **0**, always; **treat everybody** scores `prevalence − (1−prevalence) × pt/(1−pt)`.

Now the demonstration. Outcome prevalence 10%. Two models:

- **Model A** — sensitivity 0.95, specificity 0.55. Discrimination index **0.750**.
- **Model B** — sensitivity 0.45, specificity 0.96. Discrimination index **0.705**.

Model A discriminates *better*. Here is the net benefit:

| Threshold `pt` | Treat all | **Model A** | **Model B** | Best |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.053 | **0.074** | 0.043 | A |
| 0.10 | 0.000 | **0.050** | 0.041 | A |
| 0.15 | −0.059 | 0.024 | **0.039** | B |
| 0.20 | −0.125 | **−0.006** | **0.036** | B |
| 0.30 | −0.286 | **−0.079** | **0.030** | B |

Read the bold negatives. **From `pt` = 0.20 upward, Model A is worse than doing nothing at all** — its net benefit is below the zero of "treat nobody". And Model A is the one with the better discrimination.

✱ So: a model can be well calibrated, well discriminating, externally validated, and still actively harmful at the threshold you would actually use. **AUC cannot see this. Only the decision framing can.** Which is why "what would change?" is a methodological question, not a rhetorical one.

## Section 3 · The off-policy problem

Here is the deep difficulty of shape 6, and it has no fully satisfying solution.

To evaluate a policy you need to know what happens when you follow it. But your data were generated by clinicians following a *different* policy — their own. You observe the outcomes of the actions they took, and **nothing at all** about the actions they did not take.

So when a model recommends something clinicians rarely did, you have almost no data on what happens next. And the estimate of that policy's value is extrapolation dressed as measurement.

**The AI Clinician** is the case. Reinforcement learning on intensive-care records produced a policy for vasopressors and fluids, reported as outperforming clinicians. The methodological criticisms that followed converge on off-policy evaluation:

- The comparison relied on **importance weighting**, which becomes unstable exactly where the learned policy diverges most from observed practice — i.e. where the interesting recommendations are.
- **Confounding by indication is total.** Clinicians chose treatments *because* of unmeasured severity. The model sees the treatment and the outcome and cannot see the reason.
- **The model was never run on a patient.** Its performance is entirely an estimate.

⚠ The general form: **an off-policy estimate is not evidence about a policy, it is a hypothesis about a policy.** The distinction matters most when the recommendation is surprising, which is precisely when it is exciting.

✱ Your epidemiological instinct is exactly the right one here. This is confounding by indication, and no amount of algorithmic sophistication removes it — only randomisation, or a setting where the assignment mechanism is genuinely known.

## Section 4 · Optimisation, which is the honest half of the shape

Not everything in shape 6 has this problem. There is a large, unglamorous, genuinely useful class where the *objective is known and the constraints are real*:

- **Where to send screening teams**, given travel cost, population and prior risk.
- **Vaccine and commodity allocation** under supply constraints.
- **Cold-chain routing and last-mile delivery** — including drone delivery, which is a working, measurable success nobody calls AI.
- **Rostering and task-shifting** under staffing limits.
- **Screening interval selection** by risk stratum.

These are operations research with better data. And they are evaluable, because the objective is explicit and the constraint set is real, so you can compare a proposed allocation with the current one on stated criteria before anyone deploys anything.

⚠ Two cautions. First, **optimising a stated objective exactly is dangerous if the objective is incomplete** — maximise cases detected per euro and you will systematically abandon remote populations, because they are expensive. That is not a bug in the solver. Second, the constraint set is usually softer than modelled: teams get sick, roads flood, fuel runs out.

## Section 5 · Reinforcement learning, and why health is hostile to it

RL learns by acting and observing consequences. Health is close to the worst possible setting for it:

| RL needs | Health provides |
|---|---|
| Cheap exploration | Exploration means experimenting on patients |
| Many episodes | One life per patient |
| Fast, clear reward | Outcomes are delayed, censored and contested |
| A stationary environment | Practice, pathogens and policy all change |
| A known assignment mechanism | Confounding by indication everywhere |

So RL in health is almost always **offline** RL — learn from logged data, never explore — which returns us to Section 3.

✱ Where the analogy does work: settings with genuine repetition, low stakes per action and fast feedback. Appointment reminders, message timing, triage of an administrative queue. Unglamorous, and appropriately so.

## Section 6 · What this shape is actually worth

**Real and demonstrated:** logistics and allocation under explicit constraints; risk-based targeting where the alternative is uniform coverage; decision *support* that surfaces information a clinician then acts on.

**Real and under-used:** ✱ **making the objective function explicit.** The most valuable output of a shape-6 exercise is frequently not the optimum but the argument that produced it — being forced to write down what you are maximising, and for whom. Programmes routinely discover they disagree about the objective only when someone tries to encode it.

**Overstated:** autonomous treatment policy learned from observational data; anything whose evidence is an off-policy estimate presented as a result; and any "optimal" allocation whose objective function is not stated in the paper.

## Key insight

**The objective function is a policy document.** It states whose outcomes count, how much a missed case costs relative to an unnecessary intervention, and which populations are worth reaching. Writing it in code does not make it a technical artefact — it makes it a value judgement that is harder to see and harder to contest.

And the evaluative companion: **a decision system's evidence must come from decisions.** If the only evidence is an estimate of what would have happened, you have a hypothesis.

## Worked example — the decision curve that kills a good model

Dataset: a simulated cohort of 10,000 with a 10% outcome, and the two operating points above. No modelling — the point is arithmetic that any spreadsheet can carry and almost no paper reports.

### In R

```r
library(tidyverse)

prev <- 0.10          # outcome prevalence in the population you would act on

# ---- Net benefit -----------------------------------------------------------
# pt is the threshold probability: the risk at which you would act.
# It encodes the exchange rate between a missed case and an unnecessary
# intervention -- pt = 1/(N+1) if you would accept N unnecessary interventions
# to catch one true case. It is ELICITED, not tuned.
net_benefit <- function(pt, sens, spec, prev = 0.10) {
  tp <- prev * sens                 # true positives per person
  fp <- (1 - prev) * (1 - spec)     # false positives per person
  tp - fp * (pt / (1 - pt))
}

# The two anchors. "Treat nobody" is exactly zero, always -- that is the bar
# any model must clear to be worth having at all.
nb_treat_all  <- function(pt, prev = 0.10) prev - (1 - prev) * (pt / (1 - pt))
nb_treat_none <- function(pt) 0

# ---- Two models. A discriminates BETTER. ----------------------------------
A <- c(sens = 0.95, spec = 0.55)    # index (sens+spec)/2 = 0.750
B <- c(sens = 0.45, spec = 0.96)    # index                = 0.705

tibble(pt = c(0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30)) |>
  mutate(
    treat_all  = nb_treat_all(pt),
    treat_none = 0,
    model_A    = net_benefit(pt, A["sens"], A["spec"]),
    model_B    = net_benefit(pt, B["sens"], B["spec"]),
    best       = pmap_chr(list(treat_all, model_A, model_B),
                  \(ta, a, b) c("treat all","model A","model B","treat none")[
                    which.max(c(ta, a, b, 0))])
  )
#>      pt treat_all treat_none model_A model_B best
#>    0.05     0.053          0   0.074   0.043 model A
#>    0.10     0.000          0   0.050   0.041 model A
#>    0.15    -0.059          0   0.024   0.039 model B
#>    0.20    -0.125          0  -0.006   0.036 model B
#>    0.30    -0.286          0  -0.079   0.030 model B
#
# Model A has the BETTER discrimination and NEGATIVE net benefit from pt = 0.20.
# Negative means worse than treating nobody: you would do less harm by
# switching the model off. No summary of discrimination can tell you this.

# ---- The question to ask of any risk score -------------------------------
# At the threshold your programme would actually use, does it beat BOTH
# reference strategies? Plot the curves rather than quoting one number.
grid <- tibble(pt = seq(0.01, 0.5, by = 0.01)) |>
  mutate(treat_all = nb_treat_all(pt),
         model_A   = net_benefit(pt, A["sens"], A["spec"]),
         model_B   = net_benefit(pt, B["sens"], B["spec"]))

grid |>
  pivot_longer(-pt, names_to = "strategy", values_to = "net_benefit") |>
  ggplot(aes(pt, net_benefit, colour = strategy)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = 2) +      # "treat nobody"
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.9) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(-0.1, 0.12)) +
  labs(x = "threshold probability", y = "net benefit",
       title = "A model is only useful where its curve is above BOTH references")
```

⚠ The net-benefit table **was computed** and is exact. The R has not been executed here.

## Connection to the course spine

Shape 6's debt is **an account of how it was evaluated without being deployed** — and this lesson is why that debt is unusually hard to pay. In every other shape you can hold out data. Here the data record a different policy, so holding it out does not help.

The spine's second half reaches its strongest form here. Model A is better by every measure of discrimination and is worse than switching the machine off. The model is not the thing that decides whether it works — and in this shape, neither is the calibration. **The decision framing is.**

## Retain long-term

- A threshold is a value judgement: `pt = 1/(N+1)` if you would accept N unnecessary interventions per case caught. Elicited, not tuned.
- A vendor's fixed threshold makes your value judgement for you.
- Net benefit: `TP/n − (FP/n) × pt/(1−pt)`. "Treat nobody" is exactly 0 and is the bar to clear.
- A model with **better** discrimination can have **negative** net benefit — worse than switching it off. AUC cannot see this.
- Off-policy evaluation: your data record the policy clinicians followed, and nothing about the actions they did not take.
- Confounding by indication is total in treatment-policy learning, and no algorithm removes it.
- An off-policy estimate is a hypothesis about a policy, not evidence about it.
- Health is a hostile setting for RL: exploration means experimenting on patients, and the environment is non-stationary.
- Optimisation with an explicit objective and real constraints is the honest, evaluable half of this shape.
- The most valuable output is often the argument that forced you to write the objective down.
